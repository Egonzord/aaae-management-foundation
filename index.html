01

The “me” of it all

Being present for others means being aware of how you’re feeling and how you’re showing up.

Me and all my feelings

Start by noticing.

If any of these sound familiar, you’re in good company. These reactions are human. The goal is to notice them early enough to choose what you do next.

  • I often notice physical signs in my body when I am stressed or overwhelmed.
  • When I feel misunderstood, I might get frustrated or defensive.
  • I sometimes regret how I responded once my emotions cool off.
  • I often feel like I’m not qualified (or “good enough”) to be in the room.

These feelings show up quickly

The Ladder of Inference

Research suggests we draw conclusions from initial observations within about two seconds. Chris Argyris’s ladder shows every step between what happened and what we do about it. Read it from the bottom up.

  1. 01Observable dataA team member sighs several times during a meeting you’re running.
  2. 02Select dataYou notice the sighs came while you presented the new schedule.
  3. 03Add meaningThey’re annoyed with the schedule.
  4. 04Make assumptionsThey think your plan is bad.
  5. 05Draw conclusionsThey don’t respect your decisions.
  6. 06Adopt beliefsThis person is going to be a problem.
  7. 07Take actionYou call them out in front of the team.

Only the bottom rung actually happened. Maybe they just had a migraine.

Me showing up

The amygdala hijack

We can’t always have a human reaction to a human thing. When the amygdala senses a threat, the emotional center of the brain takes over (Daniel Goleman). Heart rate climbs, palms sweat, fight-or-flight kicks in, and we stop thinking strategically.

Sounds likeReacting
  • Happens right away, without thought or reflection
  • Driven by gut feelings or fear
  • Controlled by old habits and ingrained patterns
  • Often defensive
Looks likeResponding
  • Takes time and some amount of pause
  • Uses the thinking part of the brain
  • Reflects a larger goal

As leaders, our job is to respond when it would be so much easier to react.

Making it happen

Three moves for the moment

01

Reset yourself.

Take a breath. Deep breathing activates the parasympathetic nervous system, which counteracts fight or flight. It slows your heart rate, lowers your blood pressure and calms the brain’s stress center.

Breathe in for four. Hold for four. Out for four.

02

Say it out loud.

Notice the trigger and get curious. There’s real power in naming what you’re thinking. Saying it out loud clears the air.

“The story I’m telling myself is…”

03

Plan for action.

Name the feeling, name the cause and choose one next step. The Feelings Wheel helps when the right word is hard to find.

Try it now

Good leadership starts with managing yourself, then choosing how to show up for someone else.

02

The “them” part

Great leadership is about knowing other people’s needs and responding to them.

A cognitive trap

We’re all naive realists.

Naive realism is the belief that the world is exactly as I experience it, and that everyone else experiences it the same way.

It’s why a message that feels perfectly clear to you can land completely differently with someone else. The fix is to stop assuming and start asking.

The assessment

Learn how your people like to work.

The workbook asks you to choose between pairs of preferences. Neither option fits perfectly, and that’s the point. The value is in seeing where you and the people you lead differ.

When “them” used to be “us”

The people don’t change. The relationship does.

Some of the hardest management relationships start out as friendships or peer relationships. Moving from peer to manager shifts the relationship in four ways.

Power

You now affect their schedule, assignments, reviews and career.

Information

You might know things you can’t share, and they know you know.

Evaluation

You used to be a colleague. Now you judge their work.

Visibility

Who you eat lunch with, who you vent to and who gets the good assignment all read as signals.

Under-correctingThe Buddy
  • Avoids hard conversations
  • Treats standards loosely for friends
  • Still vents to former peers
  • Struggles to hold people accountable
Over-correctingThe Hammer
  • Uses distance and formality to prove authority
  • Pulls rank and changes things quickly
  • Cuts off relationships
  • Damages existing trust
The goalThe Partner
  • Keeps the trust you’ve already built
  • Is honest that the roles have changed
  • Clear about standards, curious about needs
  • Fair to everyone
  1. 01

    Reset out loud.

    If you haven’t openly addressed the change, do it. Name it, talk about how it feels, share concerns and commit to making it work.

  2. 02

    Make no assumptions.

    Being a friend or peer doesn’t mean knowing how they need to be managed. Be as intentional as you would with a new direct report.

  3. 03

    Ask for input.

    When you adjust things (and you need to), ask for input and be clear about how it will shape your decisions.

  4. 04

    Find new peers.

    Others are facing the same challenges. Find people in similar roles and lean on them.

The 4 Stages of Psychological Safety

In a psychologically safe team, people feel comfortable being themselves, learning, contributing and challenging. Each stage builds on the one before it.

  1. Stage 1

    Yourself

    Bringing your interests, values and personality to discussions.

  2. Stage 2

    A learner

    Admitting what you don’t know, and being eager to learn.

  3. Stage 3

    A contributor

    Offering ideas and suggestions without fear of judgment.

  4. Stage 4

    A challenger

    Disagreeing and raising issues to get to better outcomes.

Silence can look remarkably similar to success.

Rate each statement for your team. Then ask your team to rate the same four, anonymously. The gap between your answers and theirs is where the learning is.

  4. 04

How do we do it?

Six ways to build psychological safety

01

Make it a priority.

Name the norm out loud. Tell people what kind of team climate you’re trying to build.

Try it: conversational commitments
  • If you don’t understand something, please say that out loud.
  • If you don’t agree with a claim or suggestion, please say that out loud.
  • If someone challenges your idea, please approach that with curiosity rather than defensiveness.
  • If you feel grateful for someone or some contribution, please say that out loud.
02

You start.

Admit what you don’t know and own your mistakes out loud. When a leader models fallibility, everyone else gets permission to do the same.

03

Make people speak.

Actively make room for every member of the team to speak, not just the most verbal people.

“If you could have any superpower, which one would you pick?”A low-stakes round-robin question gets everyone’s voice in the room early.

04

Gleefully accept ideas.

To build a team full of contributors, separate brainstorming from critique. Reward contribution before evaluation.

05

Reduce the risks of failure.

Normalize learning. Be vocal about the value of experiments, pilots and course correction. When and where you can, free people up to try things without risk of embarrassment or punishment.

06

Thank the messenger.

How you react to bad news decides whether you hear the next one. Thank people for raising problems, especially the ones you didn’t want to hear, before you start solving.

Don’t punish someone for telling you what you need to hear.

Before you go

Takeaways

  1. 01

    Leadership starts with “me.”

    Your first and most persistent challenge is getting really good at identifying your needs, your emotions and your response patterns.

  2. 02

    Learn intentionally about others.

    You can’t know everything about everyone you lead, but you can ask a lot of questions. Make learning part of your leadership philosophy.

  3. 03

    Psychological safety is key.

    Setting the stage for openness, transparency and honesty is hard work that shows up in every interaction with your people.

  4. 04

    This is hard.

    You’ll get something wrong, and even when you don’t, someone might not like what you do. That’s part of the job. Give yourself grace.
